Module 4 — Closed-loop control and tuning

Week of Sep 28 · Paired lecture: Swimming robots I

What you build today

The gearbox installed with a marker flag on the output, the Seeeduino wired in and commanding the ESC untethered, running continuously, then holding position, then tracking a velocity setpoint with a tuned loop.

Before you arrive

Parts Motor with shaft and sun gear from Module 2, Faulhaber 23.04:1 gearbox, M2.5×10 screws, 3D-printed plates, second vertical bracket, 3× M2.5 and 3× M2 screws, ESC from Module 3, 4x8 mm flanged bearing, Seeeduino XIAO nRF52840, its 3D-printed bracket, 3 wires
Tools Bench supply with current limit, tachometer or camera, soldering iron
Read This page in full - the speed limits below explain most of what you will hit

Reduction

The motor is connected via a custom shaft to a gearbox with a 23.04:1 reduction by Faulhaber.1 Both parts are held in place by 3D-printed plates. The output shaft of the gearbox has its own bearing on the inside of the gearbox, and is also supported mid-way on the shaft by a 4×8 mm flanged bearing. This leaves 5 mm of shaft length free for the crank to be mounted on. A flat on the shaft helps lock the rotation of the M3 screw extending through the crank.

Installing the reduction

Our sun gear is already pressed onto the motor shaft from Module 2. What is left is mating the gearbox to the motor and fixing the pair to the plates.

  1. Slide the gearbox onto the motor shaft so the sun gear engages the planets. It should seat with light hand pressure, if it resists, back off and check the sun gear is square on the shaft rather than forcing it.

  2. Fix the blue part of the gearbox: the custom screws that come with the gearbox on one side, and M2.5×10 screws on the other.

  3. Check the output turns freely by hand through a full revolution before applying power.

  4. Close the gearbox: the silver part threads onto the blue part. Don’t tighten it.

  5. Attach the second vertical bracket to the silver part of the gearbox with 3 × M2.5 screws.

  6. Secure that bracket to the bottom fuselage plate with 3 × M2 screws. The motor bracket from Module 2 is already on the same plate, so between them the two brackets set the alignment of the whole drivetrain. Check the output still turns freely once both are bolted down.

Onboard microcontroller

So far every command has come from your laptop over micro-USB. That works on the bench and nowhere else, a robot that has to move cannot stay tethered. The commander interface does not change; what changes is who talks to it. From here on the Seeeduino XIAO nRF52840 rides on the robot and sends those same commands, and your laptop talks to the Seeeduino instead of to the ESC.

  1. Solder the Seeeduino and the ESC’s Serial1: TX, RX and GND lines. In Serial communication, TX and RX are crossed, each board’s transmit line goes to the other’s receive line, as drawn in the wiring diagram in Module 3. GND is not optional: without a shared reference the two boards have no agreed voltage for what counts as a high or low bit. To do this step, you will need to desolder the existing Serial1 TX RX lines to the daughterboard.

  2. Install the Seeeduino in its 3D-printed bracket and into the robot.

  3. Load the barebone Arduino sketch onto the Seeeduino — see Software for the repository.

  4. Modify the sketch to spin the motor at 100 rad/s and confirm it on the flag you mounted on the gearbox output.

WarningYou lose the micro-USB serial link

Serial1 now goes to the Seeeduino rather than to the daughterboard, so the serial console you used in Module 3 stops working. Flashing the ESC still works — programming runs over SWD, on different pins — but every command and every value you read back now has to pass through the Seeeduino.

Plan for it: anything you still want to inspect over micro-USB, inspect before you solder these three wires.

ImportantTo be completed by the instructor

Which repository holds the barebone Seeeduino sketch, and on which branch. The nRF52 repositories currently in Software are marked outdated, so students following this page have nothing to clone.

Task sequence

  1. Mount a small flag on the gearbox output so rotation is visible and countable.
  2. Achieve continuous spinning at low speed.
  3. Achieve position control - the output holds against a hand disturbance.
  4. Tune the loop and characterise your maximum usable speed.
ImportantTo be completed by the instructor

The specific PID tuning exercise, the target performance, and the sign-off criteria are course material and are not in the manufacturing guide.

Why low speed is the hard part

Regular drone ESCs start losing synchronisation at low speed, which is exactly the regime used for water propulsion. That is the reason this course uses an FOC ESC rather than a hobby unit — see Design notes.

Going beyond 600 rad/s

Two issues limit speed: the micro-controller loop rate, and delays from the rotation sensor. Using SPI at 10 MHz and an AS5047P sensor rated for 28kRPM speeds are the required hardware changes that improves on delay. But at very high speeds, sensor lag still becomes the binding constraint, and shows up as high current draw and noise. We need to update the sensor electrical angle offset as a function of speed.

Sensor lag

Compensating sensor lag as speed increases is necessary to go above 600 rad/s without wasting current. It is currently hand-tuned every 100 rad/s, and a fitted function is used on the robot. This could be automated by ramping through the speeds and minimising phase current — future work. This SimpleFOC forum thread is useful on the topic.

NoteCheckpoint

Continuous rotation resulting in frequencies of 1 Hz all the way to 8 Hz without loss of synchronisation, position hold against a hand disturbance, and a recorded loop frequency from F. Do not exceed 8 Hz.

MCU speed

The goal is to maximise main loop frequency, which reached 13 kHz. Read the current loop frequency with the commander’s F command.

motor.motion_downsample = 3 +0.8 kHz loop frequency
