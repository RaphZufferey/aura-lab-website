Module 3 — Motor driver, sensor and serial

Week of Sep 21 · Paired lecture: Bird-scale flapping design

What you build today

Let’s drive our motor! We aim to get a flashed G431 ESC talking to your laptop via a microcontroller, with the angular sensor wired on SPI and the motor spinning under commander control. Lots of soldering.

Work through the module with this diagram open: every step below either wires one of these links or configures it in firmware.

Before you arrive

Parts needed B-G431B-ESC1 board, AS5047P encoder, encoder mount, cables
Tools Soldering iron, tweezers, micro-USB cable, power source
Read This page, plus Design notes on why we use an FOC ESC

Electronic Speed Controller

The B-G431B-ESC1 is ST’s reference three-phase motor driver: a complete ESC on one small board, sold as an evaluation kit. It contains: - An STM32G431 microcontroller. A 170 MHz Cortex-M4 with hardware floating point. Motor control recomputes the field orientation every control loop. This MCU is the reason we can. - A three-phase inverter. Six power MOSFETs drive the three motor phases. - A detachable ST-Link programmer (the daughterboard). The small daughter board is how we program the MCU over USB, and it also carries a serial port back to your laptop. When snapped-off, the MCU cannot be reprogramed without physically resoldering it.

It also brings out many input/outputs (for exampleJ8), a CAN transceiver and a potentiometer input, none of which we use as intended. In this build J8 gets repurposed for serial, and the encoder arrives on SPI instead. Both of those reworks are below. The board is rated well above what the wing motor draws (40A), so current is not the constraint here.

Get familiar with every component on the board, how six MOSFETs turn one DC rail into three rotating phases, what current it can actually deliver, and why the programmer is a separate snap-off board under The G431 ESC board.

Encoder or Angular sensor

Why the motor needs an encoder

Field-Oriented Control works by holding the stator’s magnetic field roughly 90° ahead of the rotor’s magnets, which is the orientation that produces the most torque per amp. To place the field there, the controller has to know where the rotor is right now.

Ordinary drone ESCs infer this from back-EMF, i.e the voltage the spinning rotor induces in the phase that is not currently being driven (?@eq-bemf in Module 2). That voltage is proportional to speed, so as the motor slows the signal sinks into the noise and the estimate falls apart. This causes loss of synchronisation at low speed described in Design notes. The AS5047P removes the guess. It measures the field of the magnet bonded to the shaft end and reports the absolute angle — valid at standstill just as much as at 15 000 rpm, and correct immediately at power-on with no homing move.

What SPI is, and why we run it at 10 MHz

We will use an AS5047P encoder. The AS5047P reports angle over SPI, a four-wire synchronous bus: the controller drives the clock (SCLK), data goes out on MOSI and comes back on MISO, and CS selects the chip. Transfer speed is limited only by how fast the two ends can shift bits. We use SPI because it offers 10 MHz communication. The reason speed matters is that the angle has to be re-read every single control loop, and a stale angle is bad: the controller ends up placing the stator field where the rotor was, not where it is. That error shows up as wasted current and audible noise. The same 10 MHz clock is why the wiring has to stay short, see below.

Wire it to the board

There is no SPI link exposed directly on the ESC board. The solution is to use the SPI3 bus, exposed through a few hardware modifications and matching software changes. Figure 1 shows the four signals in context, including the MOSI tap on R14.

WarningYou lose the micro-USB serial link

Serial2, used by the daughterboard for serial communication will not work anymore as we are using those pins for SPI3 for the encoder instead. Flashing the ESC still works (programming runs over SWD, on different pins) but every command and every value you read back now has to pass through serial1.

The encoder needs power as well as the four bus signals — six wires in total:

Note that 3.3 V and GND come from different connectors. J3 carries the three bus signals and a ground, but its only supply pin is 5 V from the break-away BEC — too high for the AS5047P. The 3.3 V rail is exposed on J4, the SWD header, alongside SWDIO and SWCLK. Take power from there and ground from J3, and check both against Figure 3 before soldering.

WarningKeep the SPI cables under 8 cm

Cables between encoder and ESC need to be less than 8 cm. At 10 MHz longer runs start corrupting angle readings, and the symptom is a motor that behaves erratically rather than a sensor that obviously fails.

Next, use M2 screws to mount the encoder on the 3D printed bracket that sits around the motor. Check that the distance between sensor and magnet is 0.5 mm, and centered.

Onboard microcontroller

We will use a Seeeduino XIAO nRF52840 Sense to send commands to the ESC and record information from the ESC. Why? - It is small and light. 21 × 17.5 mm and 2.4 g. On a robot that has to move, every gram saved matters.. - It has wireless communication out of the box. The nRF52840 is a Bluetooth Low Energy chip and a microcontroller. Once the robot is sealed in Module 10 BLE becomes the only way commands to interact with the robot during operation — which is exactly how you retrieve a run in Module 11. - It has somewhere to put the data. 256 kB of RAM and 1 MB of flash on the chip itself are the two logging destinations used during wet testing. No SD card, no extra memory chip, nothing else to waterproof. - Onboard IMU to log the attitude of the robot without additional components. - It costs 20$. In case a failure, it can be replaced.

It programs from the Arduino IDE like any other Arduino board, but we highly recommend using Visual Studio Code + PlatformIO.

  1. Solder the Seeeduino and the ESC’s Serial1: TX, RX and GND lines. In Serial communication, TX and RX are crossed, each board’s transmit line goes to the other’s receive line, as drawn in the wiring diagram in Module 3. GND is not optional: without a shared reference the two boards have no agreed voltage for what counts as a high or low bit. To do this step, you will need to desolder the existing Serial1 TX RX lines to the daughterboard.

  2. Install the Seeeduino in its 3D-printed bracket and into the robot.

  3. Load the barebone Arduino sketch onto the Seeeduino — see Software for the repository.

  4. Modify the sketch to spin the motor at 100 rad/s and confirm it on the flag you mounted on the gearbox output.

How to program the ESC

With the encoder and motor connected to the ESC, we can start programing the ESC! Remap the pins in software for SPI use: in platformio.ini via build_flags, and by overwriting the WEAK PinMaps in pinmap_custom.h. See commit 18520ac on GitHub for details.

Firmware

The ESC is pre-flashed for you Code for the B-G431B-ESC1 motor controller, using the SimpleFOC library, is in the lab repository: FAAV-G431-ESC. See Software for all repositories.

Before you change anything in there, read How SimpleFOC works. It covers the two nested loops, what the FOC maths does each iteration, why the motor twitches at power-on, and which settings in the sketch matter — enough to read the firmware rather than guess at it.

WarningCareful when changing control mode

T means something different in each mode, position, velocity, or torque. Sending a velocity setpoint while in torque mode will do something you did not intend, with the wings attached. BE VERY CAREFUL ABOUT THIS.
