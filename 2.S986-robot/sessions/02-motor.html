Module 2 — Motor and transmission shaft

Summary

A brushless motor with a custom shaft pressed into its rotor bell, with the sun gear and the sensor magnet installed, bolted to its bracket and mounted on the motor plate. The motor is soldered to the electronic speed controller. The gearbox itself goes on in Module 4.

Requirements

Parts AS2303 KV1500 motor with pre-machined bell, custom motor shaft, sun gear from the Faulhaber 23.04:1 gearbox, 6 × 2.5 mm diametric magnet, 3D-printed magnet bracket, vertical nylon motor bracket, bottom fuselage plate, mounting screws
Tools Circlip pliers, manual arbor press, superglue, hex drivers
Read This page, and the Motor shaft section below in full before touching the motor. Brushless motors for the background

The motor — AS2303 KV1500

The AS2303 is a brushless outrunner. Read Brushless motors first.

We use the AS2303 KV1500 motor throughout the course. Its propeller horn cannot be separated from the bell, it has to be machined off in a lathe. That work, and the custom shaft, are prepared before the session.

What the KV rating means

KV is the motor’s velocity constant, quoted in rpm per volt. It says how fast the rotor spins per volt applied, with no load on the shaft:

\[\omega_0 = k_V \, U \tag{1}\]

The constant is not a marketing number — it falls out of the motor’s physics. As the rotor turns, its magnets sweep past the windings and induce a voltage that opposes the supply (the back-EMF):

\[U_\text{emf} = \frac{\omega}{k_V} \tag{2}\]

The supply voltage splits between this back-EMF and the ohmic drop across the winding resistance \(R\), so \(U = U_\text{emf} + R\,I\) and the speed under load becomes

\[\omega = k_V \left( U - R\,I \right) \tag{3}\]

which is why Equation 1 only holds at \(I \approx 0\). Draw current and the motor slows down.

The same constant fixes the torque. Electrical power in equals mechanical power out, \(U_\text{emf} I = \tau \omega\), and substituting Equation 2 gives \(\tau = I / k_V\) in SI units. Converting \(k_V\) from rpm/V to rad/s/V:

\[k_T = \frac{60}{2\pi \, k_V} \approx \frac{9.55}{k_V} \qquad \text{with} \qquad \tau = k_T \, I \tag{4}\]

with \(k_T\) in N·m/A when \(k_V\) is in rpm/V. Torque and speed constants are inverses of each other. A high-KV motor spins fast but gives little torque per amp; a low-KV motor of the same size and mass does the opposite. Both cannot be had at once — the choice is where to sit on that trade-off, and then to recover the missing torque with a reduction.

What this means for our robot

With \(k_V = 1500\) rpm/V and a 4S LiPo at its 14.8 V nominal voltage:

\[\omega_0 = 1500 \times 14.8 = 22\,200 \ \text{rpm} \qquad k_T = \frac{9.55}{1500} = 6.4 \ \text{mN·m/A}\]

6.4 mN·m/A is very little torque, too little to drive the wings directly, which is exactly why the 23.04:1 Faulhaber gearbox is there. It multiplies torque by 23.04 and divides speed by the same factor. One revolution of the gearbox output is one full flapping cycle, so

\[f_\text{flap} = \frac{\omega}{60 \, r} = \frac{22\,200}{60 \times 23.04} = 16.1 \ \text{Hz} \tag{5}\]

at no load. Under real aerodynamic and hydrodynamic loading the drive tops out near 15 200 rpm, i.e. about 11 Hz of flapping, the figure that reappears in Module 4.

Motor shaft

To pair the motor with the gearbox, the stock shaft of the brushless motor is replaced by a custom one. What is left to do is the disassembly and the press fit, which is still a delicate operation: the motor’s structure is mostly aluminium and quite thin, for weight savings.

  1. Remove the circlip on the shaft with the special pliers. Don’t lose it, nor the brass ring.

  2. Separate the rotor from the stator delicately, by hand.

  3. Press the stock shaft out of the rotor bell.

    WarningNever load the bell radially

    Press the shaft out very carefully, making sure to never apply forces radially on the bell. Use the manual press with appropriate support below the bell to avoid deforming the dome shape. Do not squeeze the bell with a vice, or even by hand.

  4. Press the sun gear onto the new shaft.

    WarningSun gear first — this order cannot be undone

    The sun gear goes on while the shaft is still free. Once the shaft is pressed into the bell, it can no longer be supported properly to press the gear on.

  5. Press the new shaft into the bell, making sure not to press on the dome-shaped part of the bell without appropriate support underneath. It is a tight fit on the bell and a clearance fit through the bearings.

Sensor magnet

The AS5047P encoder wired up in Module 3 does not touch the shaft. It reads the direction of the magnetic field from a small magnet rotating in front of it, so the magnet has to go on the motor.

Use a 6 × 2.5 mm diametrically magnetised disc magnet. Diametrically magnetised is the part that matters: the poles are across the diameter, not through the thickness, so the field seen by the sensor sweeps through a full rotation as the shaft turns. An ordinary axially magnetised disc of identical size presents the same pole to the sensor all the way round and gives no angle at all.

  1. Drop the magnet into its recess in the 3D-printed bracket. It should sit flat, fully seated, with no rocking.

  2. Centre it on the axis of rotation. The bracket locates it, but check. Keep the magnet concentric with the shaft to within 0.5 mm.

  3. Bond it with a single drop of superglue. One drop is enough.

  4. Turn the assembly through a full revolution by hand and watch the magnet. Any visible wobble means it is off-centre or not seated.

WarningOff-centre magnets are hard to diagnose later

An eccentric magnet may still produces a perfectly plausible angle reading at times.

Mounting the motor

With the shaft and the magnet done, the motor goes onto the robot plate. This happens in two stages: motor to bracket, then bracket to plate.

  1. Fix the motor to the vertical 3D-printed nylon bracket using four screws M2x5 mm through the mounting holes in the motor base. Don’t over tighten. Check that the rotor still turns freely by hand. Nylon is compliant, and overtightening can pull the bracket out of flat enough to preload the bearings. A motor that spun freely on the bench and feels stiff once bolted down needs the screws backed off and retightened more gently.

  2. Mount the bracket to the bottom fuselage plate. Before tightening, check the orientation: the shaft and sun gear must point towards the front of the robot.

Solder the motor wires to the Electronic Speed Controller

Since the pads are large, make sure to use enough heat / large tip on the soldering iron. The order of the cables UVW do not matter.

NoteCheckpoint

Shaft seated with the sun gear on it, the rotor spinning freely by hand with no wobble, the magnet bonded concentric to within 0.5 mm, and the motor bolted to its bracket and mounted on the plate - still turning freely once bolted down.
