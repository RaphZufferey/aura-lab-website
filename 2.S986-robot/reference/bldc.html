Brushless motors

Background for Module 2. Not needed at the bench — useful for your report, and for understanding why the motor behaves the way it does when you push it in Module 4.

Start with the brushed DC motor

Every result you need for a brushless motor is easier to see in a brushed one first, because the two obey the same electrical physics. The difference is how the windings get switched, not what the machine does once they are.

A DC motor is described by two constants and one resistance. Spinning the rotor induces a back-EMF proportional to speed, and passing current through the windings produces torque proportional to current:

\[U_\text{emf} = \frac{\omega}{k_V} \qquad\qquad \tau = k_T \, I \tag{1}\]

These are the same \(k_V\) and \(k_T\) derived in Module 2 — inverses of each other, not independent choices. The supply voltage splits between the back-EMF and the ohmic drop across the winding resistance \(R\):

\[U = \frac{\omega}{k_V} + R \, I \tag{2}\]

Substitute \(I = \tau / k_T\) and rearrange for torque, and the whole behaviour of the motor falls out as a straight line:

\[\tau = k_T \frac{U}{R} - \frac{k_T}{k_V R}\,\omega \tag{3}\]

Reading the torque–speed line

That line is the motor’s entire steady-state character, and it is fixed by the applied voltage. Two points define it:

  • Stall torque \(\tau_\text{stall} = k_T U / R\), at \(\omega = 0\). The rotor is held still, there is no back-EMF, and the only thing limiting current is the winding resistance. This is the largest torque the motor can produce and also the fastest way to destroy it — see Thermal limits.
  • No-load speed \(\omega_0 = k_V U\), at \(\tau = 0\). The rotor spins fast enough that the back-EMF nearly cancels the supply, so almost no current flows.

Everything the motor can do at that voltage lies on the line between them. Mechanical power is \(P = \tau \omega\), the product of a falling line and a rising one, so it is a parabola peaking at half the no-load speed with half the stall torque. Efficiency peaks somewhere else entirely — much closer to \(\omega_0\), because the copper loss that dominates at low speed scales with \(I^2\) while the useful output only scales with \(I\).

NoteThe useful consequence

Raising the voltage shifts the line outwards without changing its slope: more speed and more torque, same trade-off between them. That is why speed control is voltage control, and why a “faster” motor at fixed voltage is a lower-torque one.

What changes with a brushless motor

In a brushed motor the commutator and brushes are a mechanical switch riding on the rotor: they physically reconnect the windings as it turns, so the current always flows in whichever coil is best placed to produce torque. It works without any electronics at all, and it wears out, arcs, and limits how fast the motor can safely spin.

A BLDC motor deletes that switch. The permanent magnets go on the rotor, the windings stay on the stator in three phases, and an external controller decides which phases to energise based on where the rotor currently is. The motor becomes inseparable from its driver — this is why Module 3 exists, and why the rotor position measurement matters so much.

What this does not change is Equation 3. The torque and speed constants have the same meaning, the line has the same shape, and you size the motor the same way. What changes is that the line is no longer the whole story:

Brushed Brushless
Commutation Mechanical, self-acting Electronic, needs rotor position
Torque–speed line The real limit The ideal limit, then clipped by the driver
Wear Brushes are consumable Bearings only
Speed ceiling Brush arcing and bounce Commutation rate and sensor lag
Torque ripple Smoothed by many commutator segments Depends on drive scheme (trapezoidal vs FOC)

The envelope, not the line

The straight line assumes the supply can deliver whatever current the equations ask for. It cannot. At stall, Equation 2 gives \(I = U/R\), and with the sub-ohm winding resistance of a small outrunner that is a number in the hundreds of amps — far beyond both the ESC and the motor itself.

So the driver enforces a current limit, and because \(\tau = k_T I\) that is directly a torque limit. The usable region becomes:

  • a constant-torque region at low speed, flat at whatever current limit you set, because the ESC is holding current rather than letting the line dictate it;
  • a corner speed where the back-EMF has risen enough that the supply can no longer push that current;
  • above it, the original Equation 3 line, falling to \(\omega_0\).

The flat part is not physics, it is a decision. On our robot it is the decision that keeps the motor alive at the low speeds used for swimming, where the torque–speed line alone would happily draw enough current to cook the windings.

Thermal limits

Nothing in Equation 3 knows about heat, which is why the equations cheerfully predict operating points that will destroy the motor in seconds.

Almost all the loss you care about is copper loss in the windings:

\[P_\text{copper} = I^2 R \tag{4}\]

The square is what makes this the binding constraint. Doubling the torque doubles the current and quadruples the heating. A motor comfortable at its rated current will not survive twice that for long, even though the torque equation says it is only a factor of two.

Three practical consequences:

  1. Stall is the worst case, not the best. At \(\omega = 0\) there is maximum current and no rotation, so no airflow and no useful output — all the input power becomes heat in the windings. A motor held at stall can burn out without the shaft moving at all.
  2. Continuous and peak ratings are different numbers. A peak rating assumes seconds; a continuous rating assumes thermal equilibrium. Flapping is a reciprocating load, so the current is far from constant — what matters for heating is the RMS current over the cycle, not the peak you see on the bench supply.
  3. Iron losses grow with speed. Hysteresis and eddy-current losses in the stator scale with electrical frequency, so at the 1700 rad/s discussed in Module 4 the copper is no longer the only thing getting warm.
WarningHeat is cumulative and invisible

Current is the only symptom you can see, and it appears instantly, while the temperature it causes lags behind by tens of seconds. A run that looks fine on the ammeter can still be depositing heat faster than the motor sheds it. When tuning in Module 4, stop and touch the motor.

Why an outrunner

Brushless motors come in two arrangements. An inrunner spins a magnet rotor inside a stationary stator, the familiar shape from a drill. An outrunner inverts it: the stator sits still in the middle and the magnets are carried on a bell that rotates around the outside. Ours is an outrunner — it is the bell you machine and press the shaft into in Module 2.

The reason is torque. Torque is force times radius, and in an outrunner the magnets sit at the largest radius the motor’s diameter allows, rather than the smallest:

\[\tau = F \times r \tag{5}\]

The same tangential force from the same windings acts on a longer lever arm. It also leaves room for more magnet poles around the circumference. Together these raise \(k_T\) for a motor of the same mass, which by Equation 1 means a lower KV — more torque per amp, less speed per volt.

That is the right trade for us. The wings need torque at low speed, through a gearbox that is already multiplying by 23.04:1, and the alternative is either a larger reduction or more current for the same result. Two secondary properties help as well:

  • Rotor inertia. The rotating mass is at a large radius, so the bell has significant inertia and acts as a flywheel. Against a reciprocating flapping load that smooths the speed variation over a cycle. It also means the motor cannot change speed quickly, which is a real cost during transients.
  • Something to attach to. The bell is a large-diameter rotating surface, which is what makes the shaft press-fit in Module 2 mechanically sensible in the first place.

The costs are real: outrunners spin more slowly for a given voltage, the exposed rotating bell is a hazard and an ingress path, and cooling is worse because the heat-producing stator is wrapped inside the rotor rather than mounted against the case. The last point ties directly back to Thermal limits — an outrunner’s stator sheds heat mainly through its mount, which on our robot is a 3D-printed plate.
